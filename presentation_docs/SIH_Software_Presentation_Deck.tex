\section{Slide 2: The Problem --- Why This Matters}
%% ============================================================

\subsection*{The Situation in Space Today}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item There are currently over \textbf{27,000 man-made objects} orbiting Earth, including active satellites, dead satellites, and old rocket parts.
    \item These objects travel at speeds of \textbf{27,000 km/h} --- about 40 times faster than a bullet.
    \item In the last five years, companies like SpaceX (Starlink) have launched thousands more satellites, making space even more crowded.
    \item When two objects collide, they shatter into thousands of tiny pieces that then threaten even more satellites --- a chain reaction called the \textbf{Kessler Effect}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{presenterbox}
Use this analogy: ``Imagine a busy highway where all the cars are moving at 40 times the speed of a bullet, and nobody has a GPS, a horn, or brakes. That is low Earth orbit today. One crash can cause a chain reaction that makes all of space unusable for decades.'' This makes it real for the judges.
\end{presenterbox}

\subsection*{Real Proof: The 2009 Iridium--Cosmos Collision}

\begin{warnbox}
On \textbf{10 February 2009 at 16:56 UTC}, at \textbf{789 km above Siberia}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item An active American phone satellite called \textbf{Iridium 33} (560 kg, roughly the size of a small car) hit a dead Russian military satellite called \textbf{Cosmos 2251} (900 kg).
    \item They were moving towards each other at \textbf{14.12 km/s} (over 50,000 km/h) --- a near head-on crash.
    \item Both satellites were completely destroyed in milliseconds.
    \item The crash created over \textbf{2,000 trackable pieces of debris} and thousands of smaller, untrackable fragments that are still flying around in orbit today, threatening the International Space Station and other satellites.
    \item \textbf{This was entirely preventable} --- nobody detected the risk early enough to do anything about it.
\end{itemize}
\end{warnbox}

\begin{presenterbox}
This is the most important part of the presentation --- make the judges feel the impact of this event. Say: ``This is not a hypothetical. This actually happened. Two real satellites crashed. Both were completely destroyed. And the wreckage is still up there today, threatening the ISS astronauts every single day. Our project exists to make sure this can never happen again.'' You can also say: ``At the time, there was no system fast enough or smart enough to warn operators in time. We built Space-Guard to be that system.''
\end{presenterbox}

\subsection*{Why Existing Systems Are Not Enough}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Too slow}: Checking all 27,000 satellites against each other manually takes hours. By the time the calculation is done, there is no time to act.
    \item \textbf{Too many false alarms}: Simple distance-based systems trigger thousands of warnings per day. Operators get flooded and start ignoring them.
    \item \textbf{No automated escape plan}: Even when a risk is detected, figuring out the best engine burn to dodge it still requires hours of manual work by engineers.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{presenterbox}
Keep this short. You just need to plant the idea that current tools are slow, noisy, and manual. Space-Guard solves all three. You will explain how in the next slide.
\end{presenterbox}

\section{Slide 4: How We Built It --- Technical Implementation}
%% ============================================================

\begin{presenterbox}
This is the technical slide. You do not need to explain every formula. Just explain the \textit{idea} behind each part. Say: ``I will explain how each part of the system works. I will keep it as simple as possible.'' If the judges want more depth, they will ask.
\end{presenterbox}

\subsection{Part 1: Physics and Math}

\subsubsection*{A. Where Are the Satellites Right Now?}

Every satellite's official position data is published as a pair of text lines called a \textbf{Two-Line Element set (TLE)}. These lines contain coded numbers describing the orbit. We use a standard physics model called \textbf{SGP4} to decode these numbers and calculate exactly where in space the satellite is right now (as X, Y, Z coordinates relative to the center of Earth), and how fast it is moving.

\begin{presenterbox}
Say: ``Think of a TLE like a satellite's address. We take that address and use physics equations to calculate the satellite's exact GPS-style position at any moment in time.''
\end{presenterbox}

\subsubsection*{B. How Do We Find Which Satellites Are at Risk?}

Checking every possible pair of 27,000 satellites would mean over 360 million comparisons. That is too slow. We use a \textbf{two-step filter}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Step 1 (Fast Height Check)}: We instantly discard all pairs where the two satellites fly at completely different heights. If one satellite is at 400 km and another is at 800 km, they can never collide. This eliminates 90\%+ of pairs in milliseconds.
    \item \textbf{Step 2 (Precise Close-Pass Check)}: For the remaining pairs, we calculate the exact closest distance the two satellites will ever reach, and at what time. This pinpoints the exact ``Time of Closest Approach.''
\end{itemize}

\begin{presenterbox}
Good analogy: ``Imagine you want to find which cars on a highway might crash. First, you ignore all cars going in completely different directions. Then, for the remaining ones, you track their exact paths to find when they get closest. We do the same thing, but for satellites.''
\end{presenterbox}

\subsubsection*{C. What Is the Actual Chance of a Crash?}

Once we find a close pass, we calculate the exact mathematical probability of a collision. We consider the uncertainty in our position data (satellite locations are not perfectly known --- there is some error) and the physical size of each satellite. The result is a number, like ``1 in 50,000'' or ``1 in 5,000.'' We flag anything above ``1 in 10,000'' as dangerous.

The formula used is an established aerospace standard:
\begin{equation}
P_c \approx \frac{\text{HBR}^2}{2\sigma^2} \exp\!\left(-\frac{d_{\text{miss}}^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
\end{equation}
where HBR is the combined size of both satellites (about 10 metres), $d_{\text{miss}}$ is how close they get, and $\sigma$ is the uncertainty in our position measurements.

\begin{presenterbox}
You do not need to explain the formula. Just say: ``We calculate a precise crash probability, like a 1-in-5,000 chance of collision. This is a much more useful number than just saying the two satellites are 5 km apart, which doesn't tell you if that is dangerous or not.''
\end{presenterbox}

\subsubsection*{D. How Do We Steer the Satellite to Safety?}

We use a physics model (called the Clohessy-Wiltshire equations) that describes how two satellites move relative to each other in orbit. We then use a mathematical optimization technique (SVD --- Singular Value Decomposition) to find the single best direction and smallest possible engine push that will move the satellite far enough away to be safe.

\textbf{The 14$\times$ Fuel Savings Rule}: If you push the satellite 24 hours before the predicted collision, the satellite drifts along its orbit path and ends up 14 times further away than if you had used the same engine push just 1 hour before the collision. Acting early is dramatically more efficient.

\begin{presenterbox}
Great analogy for the 14× rule: ``It is like steering a ship. If you turn the wheel slightly 10 km before a reef, you avoid it easily. If you wait until you are 100 metres away, you have to turn hard and fast, and you might still crash. Same idea with satellites --- early action means less fuel and more safety.''
\end{presenterbox}

\subsection{Part 2: Software Architecture}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Backend (Python/FastAPI)}: The server that does all the calculations. Runs on Python. Handles live satellite data, collision screening, and escape plan generation.
    \item \textbf{Frontend (React / Web Browser)}: The interactive website/dashboard that shows everything visually --- the 3D globe, the satellite table, the collision replay, the escape plan sliders.
    \item \textbf{Smart Pre-Filter (Machine Learning)}: A simple pre-trained model that quickly scores satellite pairs before doing the full calculation, skipping obvious non-threats even faster.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Part 3: Where Do We Get the Satellite Data?}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item We pull live data from \textbf{CelesTrak} --- a free public database maintained by aerospace engineers that publishes the position data of all tracked satellites, updated multiple times per day.
    \item We cache this data locally so we do not hit rate limits and can respond instantly.
\end{itemize}

\begin{presenterbox}
Say: ``All the satellite data we use is publicly available. Space agencies share this data openly so that everyone can help keep space safe. We just built the smart software on top of it.''
\end{presenterbox}

\section{Slide 7: Roadmap --- What We Plan Next}
%% ============================================================

\begin{presenterbox}
This slide shows judges that you have a vision beyond the hackathon. Keep it brief. Say: ``We have four phases planned. We have already completed Phase 1 --- the working prototype you just saw.''
\end{presenterbox}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Phase 1 (Done --- SIH Prototype)}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Live satellite position tracking from CelesTrak public database.
        \item Two-step collision screening: fast height filter, then precise closest-pass calculation.
        \item Exact crash probability calculation (Foster/Alfano analytic method).
        \item \textbf{ML surrogate model trained on synthetic data} --- a Random Forest model trained on features like miss distance, relative speed, and satellite size to quickly pre-rank dangerous pairs before running the expensive full calculation. This means the expensive physics runs only on the top candidates, not every pair.
        \item Automatic escape plan generation using Clohessy-Wiltshire orbital mechanics.
        \item Interactive 3D globe visualization in the browser.
        \item Validated end-to-end on the real 2009 Iridium-Cosmos crash data --- the system independently flags it as Critical 48 hours before impact.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Phase 2 (Next 6 Months)}: Scale to the full 27,000+ satellite catalog using GPU-accelerated processing. Integrate official position uncertainty data (CDMs from Space-Track) to replace the assumed uncertainty with real measured values, making crash probability numbers even more accurate.

    \item \textbf{Phase 3 (1 Year)}: Handle entire commercial constellations (e.g., all of Starlink at once). Coordinate maneuvers so multiple satellites can dodge simultaneously without accidentally getting in each other's way. Support satellites with continuous low-thrust electric engines.

    \item \textbf{Phase 4 (2 Years)}: Full integration with space agency mission control standards. Direct telemetry handoff to operators. Real-time 24/7 monitoring service.
\end{itemize}

\begin{presenterbox}
If a judge asks ``Is this production-ready?'' say: ``Not yet --- this is a prototype. But the core physics, math, and software are all working correctly on real data. Phase 2 and beyond would bring this to operational readiness with proper infrastructure and funding.''
\end{presenterbox}
